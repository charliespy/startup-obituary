Mustafa Suleyman co-founded DeepMind. He helped build one of the most important AI labs in history. Google paid $500 million for it.

So when he started Inflection AI in 2022, investors didn't ask many questions. They just wrote checks.

$1.3 billion in checks, to be precise.

The product was Pi — short for "personal intelligence." A chatbot designed to be warm, empathetic, conversational. Not a productivity tool. Not a search engine. A companion.

"Think of it as the AI that actually cares about you," Suleyman told interviewers. The vision was compelling. The execution was polished. Pi could hold a conversation. It remembered context. It felt, for a few minutes at least, like talking to someone who genuinely wanted to help.

The problem was that nobody could figure out why they'd come back tomorrow.

Pi had users. It did not have retention. People would try it, enjoy the experience, and then return to ChatGPT to actually get things done. The warmth was nice. But warmth doesn't close tabs or replace Google.

Inside Inflection, the tension was growing. They'd raised $1.3 billion. They had roughly 100 employees. The math was simple and terrifying: each employee represented $13 million in investor capital. The pressure to ship something — anything — that justified the valuation was immense.

Then Microsoft called.

Not to buy the company. That would trigger antitrust scrutiny. Instead, Microsoft hired Suleyman as CEO of a new consumer AI division. They hired most of Inflection's key researchers. And they paid Inflection $650 million in "licensing fees" for technology that Microsoft's own teams had arguably already surpassed.

The remaining Inflection employees woke up one morning to discover their CEO, their CTO, and most of their colleagues had left for the company that was also their largest investor.

"It was like watching someone sell a house for parts while you're still living in it," one remaining employee told Bloomberg.

Inflection still exists, technically. It pivoted to enterprise AI. New CEO. New strategy. New everything except the cap table, which still reflects $1.3 billion worth of someone else's ambition.

The startup that was supposed to build the future of personal AI instead became a case study in something simpler: what happens when the founder gets a better offer.

Microsoft got the team. Investors got licensing fees. Pi got abandoned.

You can't make this up. But then again, nobody would believe you if you tried.
