When Aravind Srinivas puts money into your startup, people pay attention.

Srinivas — the CEO of Perplexity AI, one of the most talked-about AI companies on the planet — doesn't write many angel checks. So when word got out that he'd backed Astra, a small AI agents startup in India, the signal was clear: this team had something worth betting on.

Astra was building in the hottest category in AI: autonomous agents. Not chatbots that answer questions, but AI systems that actually do things — complete workflows, execute multi-step tasks, interact with software on your behalf. The agent paradigm that every major AI lab was promising but few startups had shipped. In 2023, with foundation models getting more capable by the month, the bet was that standalone AI agents could automate the tedious work that knowledge workers hate.

The team was small — roughly ten people — and operating out of India, where engineering talent is abundant and burn rates are low. They had the backing. They had the thesis. They had the timing.

They didn't have the team dynamics to survive.

By 2025, Astra shut down operations. The Inc42 report points to internal issues — the kind of founder and team problems that kill startups more reliably than bad markets or missing technology. The details are sparse, as they usually are when the cause of death is human rather than commercial. Nobody writes a blog post titled "We Shut Down Because We Couldn't Get Along."

But the pattern is one of the oldest in startups. A small team, high pressure, a market moving at lightspeed, and the interpersonal fractures that were manageable at the start becoming catastrophic under stress. Co-founder disagreements about direction. Conflicts over equity. Clashing visions of what the product should be. The specific flavor of dysfunction doesn't matter — the outcome is always the same.

What makes Astra's death notable is the context. They weren't building in a dying market. AI agents in 2024 and 2025 attracted billions in venture funding. Every major tech company was shipping agent capabilities. The tailwind was enormous. Astra had the right idea, the right backers, and the right moment.

None of it mattered.

This is the uncomfortable truth about the "founder issues" cause of death: it's the one variable that no amount of funding, market timing, or technical talent can fix. A bad market, you can pivot away from. A technical challenge, you can engineer around. A team that's broken? There's no patch for that.

Srinivas's angel investment was a vote of confidence in the people. When the people couldn't hold together, the investment thesis collapsed with them. The AI agents market roars on — AutoGPT, CrewAI, Relevance AI, and dozens of others continue to attract attention and capital. Astra simply became another data point in the startup statistic that nobody likes to talk about: most companies die from the inside.

Ten people. A famous backer. The hottest space in tech. And a reminder that startups are, before anything else, a team sport. When the team fractures, the technology, the market, and the money become irrelevant.
